Care path

Safe distance, and a life that is not organized around harm.

Safe distance and coping is the Family Solutions path for when contact would not be safe. It is for people who have decided — or are close to deciding — that staying in contact costs more than it gives. The work is to disengage as safely as possible and to cope with what the distance brings up.

Who this path is for

  • You have tried to address the relationship, and the same harm keeps returning.
  • Contact leaves you frightened, flooded, or responsible for someone else’s stability.
  • You are considering low contact or no contact and want a plan that is firm without being theatrical.
  • You already stepped back and were not prepared for the grief, the guilt, or the way other relatives responded.

Parents stepping back from an adult child, and adult children stepping back from a parent, are both welcome here. The grief is not the same. Neither requires you to describe the other person as beyond care in order to protect yourself.

Boundary-setting

A boundary is a decision about your own participation. It is not a verdict you deliver for someone else to obey. In this work we get specific:

  • Which topics you will not discuss, and what you will do when they are raised anyway.
  • Which visits, calls, or holidays you will decline.
  • How you will respond if a limit is ignored.
  • What you will tell relatives who want a full explanation, a reunion, or a side to join.

The aim is a boundary you can keep on an ordinary Tuesday, not a speech you give once. If the relationship is too toxic to negotiate inside, the boundary may be the end of contact rather than a new set of rules for it.

Grief after estrangement

Distance can be the right decision and still feel like a loss. People grieve the parent they needed, the sibling relationship others seem to have, the holidays, and the hope that this year would be different.

Guilt often arrives dressed as a reasonable argument: if you had been kinder, stronger, or more forgiving, would it have worked? Therapy is a place to separate that question from the record of what actually happened, including what you already tried.

Staying safe

If a relationship includes threats, stalking, financial control, or violence, a coping skill is not a safety plan. We will talk about practical steps — what to document, who else may need to know, what to do if someone arrives uninvited — and we will say clearly when a situation needs legal help, a local domestic violence program, or emergency services.

If you are in immediate danger, call 911. In the U.S., call or text 988. If you are experiencing domestic violence, the National Domestic Violence Hotline is 1-800-799-7233, or visit thehotline.org. This practice does not replace those resources.

What this path will not do

  • It will not coach you to punish a relative, publish their private life, or cut them off as a tactic to make them chase you.
  • It will not tell you that staying would have been braver.
  • It will not require you to be certain before you are allowed to start. Uncertainty is a common reason to come in.

If you later decide that a careful reconnection is worth testing, the repair and reunite path is open. Distance now does not have to be a permanent identity.
